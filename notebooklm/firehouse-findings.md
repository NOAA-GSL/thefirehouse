# The Firehouse — collected project findings

Source document for the topic-area synthesis. It contains all 4 published projects in The Firehouse, NOAA Global Systems Laboratory's hub for fire weather social science. Each project's abstract, takeaways, needs and recommendations are reproduced exactly as the researchers submitted them.

Topic areas are a lens across the whole collection. No project is assigned to a topic area; a single project may inform several areas or none.

## The four topic areas

### Observations and Monitoring

What forecasters and land managers can actually see, measure, and share about fire weather conditions as they develop.

Covers:

- Surface observation networks, mesonets, and sensor coverage
- Remote sensing and satellite products used operationally
- Field reports from incident personnel and land managers
- Data sharing, formats, and access between agencies

### Forecasts and Modeling

How fire weather guidance is produced, how far ahead it reaches, and how well it matches local fuel and terrain conditions.

Covers:

- Model guidance, ensembles, and forecast skill for fire weather
- Lead times and how they line up with planning windows
- Downscaling and local adaptation of national products
- Uncertainty, probability, and how guidance is communicated

### Warnings and Immediate Response

How warnings are issued, interpreted, and handed off during active fire weather events.

Covers:

- Red flag warning criteria and issuance thresholds
- How warnings are worded, received, and interpreted
- Hand-off between forecast offices and incident command
- Evacuation, public messaging, and protective action decisions

### Strategic Adaptation and Institutional Governance

How agencies coordinate, evaluate, and adapt fire weather programs over the long term.

Covers:

- Interagency roles, responsibilities, and coordination
- Program evaluation and measures of impact
- Workforce capacity, training, and institutional knowledge
- Policy, funding, and long-term adaptation planning

## Project 1: Igniting Impact: Advancing Fire Weather Decision Support Services

- **Project ID:** R_18IGAm4T7fuN7MJ
- **Page:** /projects/igniting-impact-advancing-fire-weather-decision-support
- **Researchers:** Stephanie Hoekstra
- **Organization:** Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere
- **Completed:** 2026
- **Project type:** Research Study
- **Data collection:** Survey
- **Fire cycle phases:** Before (Pre-fire planning, prevention, and mitigation); During (Fire detection, active suppression, and downstream impacts); Long-term (Planning, adaptation, and policy)
- **Geographic areas:** National / not region-specific

### Abstract

This study evaluates how wildland fire management professionals access, perceive, and utilize meteorological decision-support information across the wildfire lifecycle. Based on a comprehensive national mixed-methods survey (n = 475), the study examined a variety of fire weather sources, prioritizing local National Weather Service (NWS) offices. Findings reveal that fire professional informational needs vary significantly by geographic region and operational role. Professionals expressed demand for extended Red Flag Warning (RFW) lead times (48 hours), dynamic two-way digital communication platforms, and probabilistic risk messaging. Notably, collaborative fuel verification prior to warning issuance correlated with a 31% increase in product trust. Overall, results indicate that fire weather support can be optimized by incorporating more fuels data into product issuance workflows, transitioning to visual probabilistic frameworks, and adopting high-touch communication protocols to enhance risk management.

### Major takeaways

- Information utility varies by region and function across diverse fire weather sources. Forecasters must engage their local partners to align specific technical tools with operational roles throughout the wildfire lifecycle.
- Incorporating partner-provided ground-truth fuel data prior to warning issuance increases RFW trust by 31%, demonstrating that collaborative data verification substantially improves product credibility and operational relevance.
- Fire professionals favor probabilistic visual metrics—specifically a combination of text and graphics—and tiered "Particularly Dangerous Situation" (PDS) headers over binary forecasts, enabling more nuanced risk assessments and scalable operational decision making.
- Fire professionals prioritize concise, event-driven updates delivered via two-way digital channels over static briefings, highlighting a shift toward dynamic, high-touch operational communication protocols.

### End-user needs and recommendations

**Entry 1**

Need: Need for role-tailored information among fire professionals. Product utility varies significantly by geographic region and operational role.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters consult local fire professionals directly to identify their specific tool preferences and tailor weather products to match end-user operational workflows.

**Entry 2**

Need: Need for collaborative fuel verification among fire professionals and NWS forecasters. Forecast products lack integration of local ground-truth fuel conditions, reducing user buy-in.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters consult local fire professionals on fuel conditions prior to issuing Red Flag Warnings to increase forecast trust and local relevance.

**Entry 3**

Need: Need for high-confidence wind and relative humidity metrics fire professionals. Operational decisions from pre-planning through active suppression rely heavily on stable, highly accurate wind speed and relative humidity forecasts.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters prioritize maximum reliability for these core variables across all product types throughout the incident lifecycle.

**Entry 4**

Need: Need for earlier signals of critical conditions among emergency managers and fire professionals. Standard 12–24 hour Red Flag Warning lead times limit proactive operational scaling before major weather events.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters utilize Fire Weather Watches and other early planning products to convey trend information and explicitly indicate the likelihood of subsequent Red Flag Warning issuance at least 48 hours in advance.

**Entry 5**

Need: Need for dynamic two-way communication between fire professionals and forecasters. Fire professionals require increased communication across all formats, especially channels that enable direct, interactive dialogue to stay ahead of rapid situational changes.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters use more direct messaging platforms to facilitate frequent, real-time interactions.

**Entry 6**

Need: Need for visual probabilistic risk data among fire professionals. Simple binary forecasts do not capture operational uncertainty or support complex risk management.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters integrate combined text-and-graphic probabilistic visual summaries into core warning products for faster scanning in the field and improved decision making.

**Entry 7**

Need: Need for tiered threat messaging among fire partners. Standard warnings fail to distinguish routine critical weather from rare, catastrophic threats.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters implement "Particularly Dangerous Situation" (PDS) headers during high-end Red Flag Warnings to signal extreme urgency.

**Entry 8**

Need: Need for discretionary warning issuance in marginal Red Flag Warning cases for fire professionals. Strict adherence to strict criteria can prevent coordination roles from obtaining the administrative cover needed to scale operations during critical events.

Recommendation: Recommend NWS forecasters exercise professional discretion to issue warnings during near-criteria events when secondary parameters indicate heightened risk, applying "marginal" or "borderline" labels to maintain credibility.

## Project 2: Fire Weather Testbed Evaluations #002: Fire Detection and Dissemination - NESDIS Next Generation Fire System and NWS Hotspot Notification Tool

- **Project ID:** R_5FKyyCLziggLdgA
- **Page:** /projects/fire-weather-testbed-evaluations-002-fire-detection
- **Researchers:** Emily Wells, Ansley Baring, Benjamin Hatchett, Stephanie Hoekstra, Laura Sample McMeeking, Kyle Thiem, Zach Tolby, Jamie Vickery
- **Organization:** Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere
- **Completed:** 2025
- **Project type:** Testbed Evaluation
- **Data collection:** Survey, Focus groups, Workshop/roundtable discussions
- **Fire cycle phases:** Before (Pre-fire planning, prevention, and mitigation); During (Fire detection, active suppression, and downstream impacts); Long-term (Planning, adaptation, and policy)
- **Geographic areas:** Northern California (ONCC); Southern California (OSCC); Rocky Mountain — CO, WY, SD, NE, KS (RMCC); Southern Area — incl. PR & USVI (SACC)

### Abstract

In June 2024, the NOAA Fire Weather Testbed conducted a five-day, in-person evaluation of the National Environmental Satellite, Data, and Information Service (NESDIS) Next Generation Fire System (NGFS) and NWS Hotspot Notification Tool (HSNT). NGFS is a satellite-based AI algorithm utilizing NOAA’s geostationary and low Earth-orbiting satellites to detect and monitor wildland fires, highlighting potential new ignitions. It is developed jointly by NESDIS and the University of Wisconsin Space Science and Engineering Center’s Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies. HSNT, developed by NWS Norman, Oklahoma, disseminates fire detection locations and weather forecasts from NWS meteorologists to land management partners via text or email notifications. Four meteorologists and five state fire partners participated in the evaluation. After receiving NGFS and HSNT training, meteorologist–land manager pairs worked together on displaced real-time simulations. Participants completed daily surveys regarding NGFS usage during simulations and joined a final focus group to evaluate overall usability and utility. FWT facilitated two recorded focus group discussions—one for meteorologists and one for fire partners—allowing each group to focus on discipline-specific applications and feedback. The FWT analyzed their feedback to share with the product development team.

### Major takeaways

- The publicly accessible NGFS provides utility as a fire detection and monitoring tool. It enhances situational awareness, serving as a safety net for forecasters. By incorporating NGFS detections, the HSNT adds capabilities for the NWS to alert partner agencies. The NGFS and HSNT should integrate effectively into NWS operational environments.
- By incorporating NGFS detections, the HSNT adds capabilities for the NWS to alert partner agencies. Evaluation findings suggested that the NGFS and HSNT can integrate effectively into NWS operational environments.
- The publicly available NGFS/HSNT notification tools were perceived as not resource intensive and therefore capable of filling needs, especially for resource-limited land management jurisdictions. Because it is publicly available, land management agencies could integrate NGFS into their own operational environments or GIS platforms.
- Participating meteorologists and land managers found that the NGFS’ high temporal resolution enhanced situational awareness for monitoring wildland fires (wildfire and prescribed).

### End-user needs and recommendations

**Entry 1**

Need: Need for “safety net” tools among meteorologists: Automated "safety net" tools (like NGFS) are needed to monitor active fires and prevent missed detections, reducing cognitive overload during operations.

Recommendation: Recommend ingesting NGFS into NWS Advanced Weather Interactive Processing System (AWIPS) with customizable display capabilities (e.g., hotspot detections overlaid with base satellite, meteorological, geographical data) and fire radiative power color-coding to help meteorologists differentiate new ignitions from known/older thermal activity.

**Entry 2**

Need: Need for adjustable alert thresholds among meteorologists: The capability for meteorologists’ to adjust and customize hotspot alert thresholds is needed to stay informed on emerging hotspots without becoming inundated during high-activity fire periods.

Recommendation: Recommend integrating confidence/uncertainty flags and customizable local alert thresholds pre-determined with local land management partners.

**Entry 3**

Need: Need for contextual data and weather overlays among land managers: Land managers need contextual data, confidence indicators, and representative weather observations alongside hotspot detections to validate thermal anomalies and reduce concerns over false positive detections.

Recommendation: Recommend training materials and documentation that explain the NGFS process (e.g., how it detects fires, integrates known wildland fire information, and incorporates uncertainty) to support land managers in validating detections with other sources.

**Entry 4**

Need: Need for controlled fire detection information flows among meteorologists and land managers: Both groups need information flows to notify land management partners about critical hotspot detections without overwhelming them with unnecessary data.

Recommendation: Recommend adding a confirmation mechanism to “close the loop” between meteorologists and partner agencies to confirm receipt of NWS-provided hotspot notification(s).

**Entry 5**

Need: Need for multi-modal communication options between meteorologists and land managers: Land managers need flexible, multi-modal communication options (including via text message) to ensure time-sensitive hotspot alerts reach field partners reliably despite internet connectivity constraints.

Recommendation: Recommend ensuring multiple communication pathways are available for agency partners to use to send and receive NGFS/HSNT as needed.

## Project 3: Fire Weather Testbed Evaluations #003 - Interagency Collaboration: Tactical Integrated Warning Team (IWT) for Wildland Fire Operations

- **Project ID:** R_5zjcvv7ibzYt735
- **Page:** /projects/fire-weather-testbed-evaluations-003-interagency
- **Researchers:** Emily Wells, Ansley Baring, Benjamin Hatchett, Stephanie Hoekstra, Laura Sample McMeeking, Kyle Thiem, Zach Tolby, Jamie Vickery
- **Organization:** Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere
- **Completed:** 2025
- **Project type:** Testbed Evaluation
- **Data collection:** Survey, Focus groups, Workshop/roundtable discussions
- **Fire cycle phases:** Before (Pre-fire planning, prevention, and mitigation); During (Fire detection, active suppression, and downstream impacts); Long-term (Planning, adaptation, and policy)
- **Geographic areas:** Northern California (ONCC); Southern California (OSCC); Rocky Mountain — CO, WY, SD, NE, KS (RMCC); Southern Area — incl. PR & USVI (SACC)

### Abstract

In June 2024, the NOAA Fire Weather Testbed conducted a five-day evaluation of the Tactical Integrated Warning Team (IWT) for fire operations. First implemented in the Southern Great Plains, the Tactical IWT involves regular coordination between meteorologists and fire managers before and during wildland fire incidents to collaboratively assess fire risk, monitor ongoing fire activity, and, when necessary, issue Fire Warnings. Although the Tactical IWT approach has improved collaboration, situational awareness, and decision-making in the Southern Great Plains, understanding its broader applicability for wildfire response in other regions requires further study. Evaluation efforts focused on assessing the Tactical IWT organizational structure, how fire weather products are integrated for decision support, and how interagency collaboration can enable more effective fire management. Participating meteorologists and land managers were paired by state and worked together on displaced real-time simulations to mirror the Tactical IWT approach. Participants completed daily surveys and joined focus groups to evaluate overall impressions of the Tactical IWT approach, including regional applications. FWT facilitated two recorded focus group discussions—one for meteorologists and one for fire partners—allowing each group to focus on discipline-specific feedback to share with product teams.

### Major takeaways

- Participating meteorologists and land managers believed the Tactical IWT approach, both before and during wildland fire incidents, has the potential to improve communication, coordination, and situational awareness among meteorologists, land managers, and other fire/emergency response partners, thus enabling unified public messaging and coordinated response to wildland fire threats.
- Participating meteorologists found value in and advocated for the real-time communication element of the Tactical IWT approach for fire operations. Participating meteorologists described the IWT as a “force multiplier” that can enhance inter-agency communication and public communication beyond annual meetings/tabletops.
- Land managers had experience working in collaborative team structures with similarities to IWTs, which could help implement Tactical IWTs for fire operations. Land managers desired timely and accurate meteorological information to support operational response, planning, and preparedness decisions, sharing that interagency relationships established through Tactical IWTs could fill information gaps.
- Both meteorologist and land manager participants expressed concerns in implementing and maintaining the Tactical IWT for fire operations.

### End-user needs and recommendations

**Entry 1**

Need: Need for Integrated Warning Team implementation framework among meteorologists and land managers: Participants foresaw challenges in building, implementing, and maintaining Tactical IWTs, driven by ambiguity regarding partner responsibilities, authority, and inclusion. Participants need pre-determined guidelines scaled locally.

Recommendation: Recommend using the Southern Great Plains IWT approach to develop frameworks with clearly defined–yet locally adaptive/scaleable–partner roles and representation. Determine whether/how to formally institute (e.g., via state Annual Operating Plans).

**Entry 2**

Need: Need to address capacity constraints among meteorologists and land managers: Both participant groups expressed a need to address workforce capacity constraints, as staffing/bandwidth constraints may present barriers to IWT implementation and maintenance.

Recommendation: Recommendations include that Tactical IWT frameworks consider localized capacity and offer implementation strategies for varying levels of partner engagement.

**Entry 3**

Need: Need to address potential barriers to organizational “buy in” among land managers: Participating land managers expressed a need to address potential barriers related acquiring organizational “buy in” (i.e., from those with “boots on the ground”). They shared the importance of demonstrating the value of Tactical IWTs for fire operations.

Recommendation: Recommend framing Tactical IWTs as flexible decision support and situational awareness tools that fit partner needs, capacities, and workflows.

**Entry 4**

Need: Need for Tactical IWT flexibility and scalability among meteorologists and land managers: Land managers need compatibility between Tactical IWTs and the National Incident Management System’s Incident Command System (ICS), which is scalable by design. Similarly, meteorologists need the framework to be adaptable and scalable on a regional basis to meet operational contexts.

Recommendation: Recommend ensuring Tactical IWTs are customizable and scalable based on local needs while aligned with the ICS.

**Entry 5**

Need: Need for local IWT training, coordination, and relationship building between meteorologists and land managers: Meteorologists and land managers need to establish collaborative, trusted relationships and operational procedures before interagency collaboration is required during an incident. Land managers felt this can help inter-agency and multi-jurisdiction alignment, building situational awareness and thus trust/confidence.

Recommendation: Recommend consistent and coordinated training involving local meteorologists and fire partners.

## Project 4: Igniting Insight: Evaluating NWS Fire Weather Products within a Fire Partner Decision Making Context

- **Project ID:** R_6LG6UbI2AXyQUtS
- **Page:** /projects/igniting-insight-evaluating-nws-fire-weather-products
- **Researchers:** Stephanie Hoekstra, Benjamin Hatchett, Jamie Vickery
- **Organization:** Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere
- **Completed:** 2025
- **Project type:** Research Study
- **Data collection:** Focus groups
- **Fire cycle phases:** Before (Pre-fire planning, prevention, and mitigation); During (Fire detection, active suppression, and downstream impacts)
- **Geographic areas:** National / not region-specific

### Abstract

This study evaluates how National Weather Service (NWS) partners (Fire Chiefs, Fire Analysts, Emergency Managers, etc.) utilize fire weather sources across the decision timeline—from long-range planning weeks in advance to tactical response during active events—focusing mostly on Red Flag Warnings (RFWs) and Fire Warnings. Based on 19 focus groups with 85 participants across six NWS regions, RFWs are widely trusted throughout the planning timeline to build situational awareness, validate decisions, and justify resources, serving a supportive rather than initiating role. Conversely, tactical Fire Warnings face partner skepticism during rapid-onset events, with untrained partners citing fears of bureaucratic delays and jurisdictional friction. To improve utility across all decision phases, participants recommended RFW refinements—incorporating localized fuel data, cross-office consistency, and trend information—alongside a flexible framework for Fire Warnings.

### Major takeaways

- Red Flag Warnings validate existing decisions, elevate situational awareness, and justify resources, but they serve a supportive rather than initiating role in operational fire management.
- Enhancing Red Flag Warning utility requires incorporating localized fuel data, increasing cross-office forecasting consistency, and delivering clear trend information to support complex decision timelines.
- Untrained partners view NWS Fire Warnings with skepticism—fearing administrative delays and jurisdictional friction—highlighting the need for modular training and clear implementation frameworks.
- Rapidly escalating wildfires create persistent lead-time and redundancy challenges for Fire Warnings, necessitating Tactical Integrated Warning Teams and WEA integration to improve public alerts.

### End-user needs and recommendations

**Entry 1**

Need: Need for clear and detailed forecast discussions. Forecast discussions are highly valued for providing insight into forecaster reasoning, allowing fire partners to interpret confidence levels, trends, and nuances not captured in standard forecasts.

Recommendation: Recommend expanding the deliberate use of Forecast Discussions during critical fire weather periods to explicitly highlight forecaster confidence, forecast rationale, and key weather trends (particularly whether a Red Flag Warning is expected in upcoming days).

**Entry 2**

Need: Need for consistent and transparent incorporation of fuels data in RFWs. There is a clear inconsistent use of fuels in Red Flag Warning issuance. Fire partners want clear, documented incorporation of fuel receptiveness into warning decisions.

Recommendation: Recommend incorporating fuels data into RFW issuance more systematically and develop clearer national guidance on fuel integration.

**Entry 3**

Need: Need for better cross-office coordination in product issuance. Jurisdictional boundaries can lead to mismatches in warnings (whether issued at all/criteria/lead time) across neighboring areas. Fire partners want greater alignment across WFOs to ensure cohesive messaging and decision-making.

Recommendation: Recommend encouraging greater coordination between adjacent WFOs to align timing and criteria—especially in overlapping CWAs or regions with prescribed fire operations.

**Entry 4**

Need: Need for inclusion of trend and duration information in RFWs. Fire partners are planning around when conditions will peak, how long they’ll last, and whether a warning spans multiple days. Current RFWs sometimes lack this critical trend detail.

Recommendation: Recommend that forecasters indicate how long critical conditions are expected to last, when they will peak, and whether a multi-day event is likely.

**Entry 5**

Recommendation: Recommend hosting regular (biannual or annual to align with Annual Operating Plans) fire partner-forecaster coordination meetings that focus on evaluating and refining RFW/Fire Weather Watch criteria based on real-world impacts.

**Entry 6**

Recommendation: Recommend acknowledging that RFWs serve public communication, validation, and legitimacy roles—even if they are not the initial trigger for decisions. This has changed from their original intent and this description should be updated in official NWS communications and documentation.

**Entry 7**

Need: Need for clearer differentiation and dissemination of Fire Warnings among public safety partners Confusion over how Fire Warnings relate to Red Flag Warnings and evacuation orders raises concerns about public misunderstanding.

Recommendation: Recommend clarifying alert distinctions and transitioning Fire Warnings to Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)—leveraging geofenced mobile reach to deliver critical protective actions to mobile populations during imminent threats where traditional Emergency Alert System channels fail to reach them.

**Entry 8**

Need: Need for consistent authority and decision-making protocols for Fire Warnings among multi-agency partners Jurisdictional complexities create uncertainty over who initiates Fire Warnings during multi-agency incidents.

Recommendation: Recommend establishing frameworks that define clear issuance roles among NWS, fire management, and law enforcement, potentially via Integrated Warnings Teams—supported by interagency training—and developing automated request processes to reduce cognitive burden and operational delays during high-stress events.

**Entry 9**

Need: Need for regional flexibility in Fire Warning implementation. Fire partners emphasized that Fire Warnings may not be appropriate in all areas, especially where effective local systems are already in place or fire risk is lower.

Recommendation: Recommend establishing flexible regional implementation guidelines that allow NWS offices and local public safety agencies to opt into or customize Fire Warning workflows based on existing local capabilities and risk profiles.
